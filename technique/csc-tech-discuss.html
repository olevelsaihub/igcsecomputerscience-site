Computer Science / Discuss (6 marks)

Pearson Edexcel · International GCSE Computer Science · 4CP0

Discuss (6 marks)

Pick out the issue in the question, explore its different sides and develop each idea with reasoning. Any bullet points in the question show areas you are expected to cover.

Command word: DiscussUsually 6 marksAbout 7-8 minutes, including a quick plan.

What Pearson asks

Pick out the issue in the question, explore its different sides and develop each idea with reasoning. Any bullet points in the question show areas you are expected to cover.

How the marks are earned

Marked by levels, not by ticks. Low marks go to separate facts with little linking. The middle band needs adequate knowledge with some lines of reasoning. The top band needs accurate, relevant computer science used to build a sustained, well-organised argument where ideas lead into each other. A list of many short points stays in the low band however long it is.

Method

  1. Underline the issue and any bullet points; plan three or four ideas that cover them.

    Why: Covering every area the question names is needed for the higher levels.

  2. For each idea, name the technology precisely (sensor, GPS, app, licence, encryption).

    Why: Accurate subject knowledge is what the level descriptors reward first.

  3. Develop each idea: how it works, then what effect it has for the people in the question.

    Why: Linked reasoning moves the answer from basic to developed.

  4. Include at least one limit, risk or other side.

    Why: Exploring different aspects shows a real discussion, not a one-sided list.

  5. Use paragraphs and linking words (because, this means, however, as a result).

    Why: A clear, logical structure is part of the top-level description.

  6. Finish with a short sentence pulling the ideas together.

    Why: It keeps the line of reasoning sustained to the end.

Answer frame

Idea 1 [technology] -> how it works -> this means... -> effect. Idea 2 ... Idea 3 ... However, [limit/other side] ... Overall...

Common errors

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Writing a bullet list of features with no explanation of their effect.
  • Ignoring one of the bullet points or areas named in the question.
  • Using general words ('it helps you') instead of naming the technology.
  • Presenting only one side with no limits or drawbacks.
  • Going off-topic, for example into cost or fashion when the question is about health or privacy.

Worked illustration

June 2025 · Paper 1 · Q5(c)

The complete original question is shown; the illustration answers part (c).

Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A smartwatch has built-in sensors that measure things like pulse and how well the wearer is sleeping. Because these numbers are collected all day, a user can spot a change, such as a resting heart rate that keeps rising, and see a doctor early.

The watch also uses its accelerometer and GPS to count steps and record the route and distance of a run. This turns exercise into numbers the athlete can track week by week, so they can set targets and see progress, which encourages them to keep training. If the wearer sits still for too long, the watch can buzz to remind them to move.

The data is sent wirelessly to a phone app, which can analyse it in more depth and show trends as charts. For elderly or vulnerable people, a watch that detects a fall and sends an alert means they can live more independently while still being safe.

However, the benefit depends on the person acting on the information. Some users stop paying attention to the readings after a while, and the sensors are not as accurate as medical equipment. Overall, wearable technology can improve health by giving people regular feedback and early warnings, as long as they use it consistently.

Why it earns the marks

Each paragraph names a specific technology (sensors, accelerometer, GPS, wireless link to an app) and follows it through to a health effect, so the reasoning is linked rather than listed. A limits paragraph shows the issue has been explored from more than one side, and the closing sentence ties the argument together, matching the top-level description.

Question types that use this card

  • Cloud storage: what it is, pros, cons and security
  • Translators: assembler, compiler and interpreter
  • Finding and fixing weaknesses: penetration testing, ethical hacking, code reviews, audit trails
  • Protecting software by law: copyright, patents, trademarks and licences
  • Ethics of technology: privacy, inclusion and professionalism
  • Technology, the environment and health
  • Emerging tech: quantum, DNA computing, AI and nanotechnology
