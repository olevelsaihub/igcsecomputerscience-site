Protecting software by law: copyright, patents, trademarks and licences

Read the narration

Idea. If you wrote a song, you would not want someone else to sell copies and keep the money. Software is the same. A creation of the mind that belongs to its creator is called intellectual property. The law gives four kinds of protection. Copyright protects the code. A patent protects an invention. A trademark protects a brand name or logo. And a licence sets out how the software may be used. Copyright is automatic. It applies as soon as the work is created, with no need to apply. A patent must be applied for, and lasts for a fixed time. A trademark is registered for a name or logo. Buying software usually buys a licence to use it, not ownership. A proprietary licence stops users seeing, changing or sharing the source code. An open-source licence lets them study, change and share it. Creative Commons lets creators set their own conditions. Software can also be protected from illegal copying with a licence key, and with online registration or activation. A copied version then will not work on another computer.

Method. Name the protection, and say what it protects. Explain how licensing controls use, with keys, terms and registration. In a discuss answer, write a paragraph for each bullet, link it to the developer, then conclude.

Worked example. Here is a real question from Paper 1 in November 2021. Akiko writes software. Discuss how it can be protected from illegal copying and distribution, considering intellectual property and licensing. It is worth six marks. Plan the answer around both bullets: intellectual property and copyright first, then licence keys and the type of licence, then a conclusion. Her programs are her intellectual property. Copyright protects her code automatically, so copying it without permission is illegal. She could patent a genuinely new process, and register a trademark for its name. Buyers get only a licence, not ownership. A unique licence key and online activation stop copies working. She chooses the licence type: proprietary to stop changes, or open source to allow sharing on her terms. To conclude, copyright gives Akiko legal rights over her work, and licence keys and terms make those rights practical. This explanation is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is giving technical measures, such as passwords or firewalls, when the question asks about legal protection. Another mistake is saying copyright must be applied for. It is automatic. And buying software does not mean you own it.

Your turn. Now you try. Leo writes all the code for his phone app himself, and designs a new name and logo for it. Which legal protection covers the code, and which covers the name and logo? Pause here and work it out.

Answer. The code is protected by copyright, automatically, as soon as Leo writes it. The name and logo can be protected by a trademark. Remember the method: name the protection, say what it protects, and explain how licensing controls use. Now try the practice questions for this type.
