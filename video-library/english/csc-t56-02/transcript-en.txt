Think of headphones. Wired ones plug in with a cable and rarely drop out. Wireless ones let you walk around, but the sound can cut out if you go too far away. Networks have the same choice: wired or wireless. Wired networks send data along cables. Copper cable carries electrical signals, and fibre-optic cable carries pulses of light. Wireless networks send data through the air as radio waves. Wi-Fi uses the two point four gigahertz and five gigahertz bands. Wired links are steadier, are not weakened by walls or distance, are harder to intercept, and often have higher bandwidth. But cables must be installed, they tie devices to one place, they can be a trip hazard, and phones have no network port. Network speed is measured in bits per second, such as megabits per second or gigabits per second. Bytes, mebibits and mebibytes are units of storage, not network speed.

First, check which way round the question goes. Then pick one feature for each point, and write it as a comparison. For explain or describe, add the result with so, or the other side with whereas. Use these features: reliability, the effect of walls and distance, security, mobility and installation. Each one gives a separate point.

Here is a real question from Paper 1 in June 2023. Describe one difference between the media used by a wired network and a wireless network. It is worth two marks. The feature is the medium the data travels through. On the wired side, packets travel along cables, as electrical signals in copper, or as pulses of light in fibre-optic cable. On the wireless side, packets travel through the air as radio waves. Join both sides with whereas, so it reads as one difference. This explanation is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is saying wireless just uses waves. Say radio waves, travelling through the air. For copper, say electrical signals, not just electricity. Another mistake is the unit of speed. Network speed is in bits per second, never bytes per second.

Now you try. Lena works on a busy train. She can use the free train Wi-Fi, or a personal hotspot from her own phone. Give two reasons the hotspot may be better. Pause here and work it out.

First, fewer people share the hotspot, so Lena gets more of the bandwidth. Second, only devices she allows can join, so it is more secure than public Wi-Fi. Remember the method: check the direction, choose one feature per point, and write each as a comparison. Now try the practice questions for this type.
