Read the narration

A light switch has only two states: on or off. A computer stores everything with tiny switches like this. On is written as one, off as zero. Each one or zero is called a bit, and eight bits make a byte. You already use place value. In our everyday denary system, three hundred and forty-five means three hundreds, four tens and five ones. Each column is worth ten times the one on its right. Binary works the same way, but each column is worth double the one on its right. For eight bits, the columns are one hundred and twenty-eight, sixty-four, thirty-two, sixteen, eight, four, two and one. Unsigned means there is no minus sign. So eight bits can hold any whole number from zero, all zeros, up to two hundred and fifty-five, all ones.

Binary to denary is adding. Write the headers, then add the place values above every one and ignore the zeros. Zero one zero zero, one zero one zero is sixty-four plus eight plus two, which is seventy-four. Denary to binary is taking away. Start at one hundred and twenty-eight and ask: does it fit into what is left? If yes, write one and subtract. If no, write zero. Then move one column right. Stop at the one column. What is left must be zero. Write all eight bits, with a space between the two nibbles. Then convert back to check.

Here is a real question from Paper 1 in June 2023. Convert the denary unsigned integer sixty to eight-bit binary. It is worth two marks. Does one hundred and twenty-eight fit into sixty? No, so write zero. Does sixty-four fit? No, write zero. Thirty-two fits. Write one. Sixty take away thirty-two leaves twenty-eight. Sixteen fits into twenty-eight. Write one, and twelve is left. Eight fits into twelve. Write one, and four is left. Four fits. Write one, and nothing is left. So the two and one columns are zero. The answer is zero zero one one, one one zero zero. Check: thirty-two plus sixteen plus eight plus four is sixty. Each nibble in the right place earns a mark. This explanation is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

The most common mistake is dropping the leading zeros. One one one one zero zero is only six bits. The question asked for eight. Another mistake is starting the headers at two hundred and fifty-six, or writing the bits backwards. Both shift every bit into the wrong column.

Now you try. Convert the denary number two hundred and one to eight-bit binary. Then convert your answer back to check it. Pause here and work it out.

One hundred and twenty-eight fits into two hundred and one, leaving seventy-three. Sixty-four fits, leaving nine. Thirty-two and sixteen do not fit, so they are zero. Eight fits into nine, leaving one. Four and two do not fit. One fits, leaving zero. The answer is one one zero zero, one zero zero one. Check: one hundred and twenty-eight plus sixty-four plus eight plus one is two hundred and one. Remember the method: write the headers, place the biggest values first, then write eight bits and check back. Now try the practice questions for this type.
