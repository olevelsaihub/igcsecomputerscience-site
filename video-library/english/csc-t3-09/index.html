When you type a message, the computer cannot store letters. It can only store binary. So everyone agrees a list that gives each character its own number. That list is called a character set. Standard ASCII uses seven bits for each character. Two to the power seven is one hundred and twenty-eight, so there are one hundred and twenty-eight codes, from nought to one hundred and twenty-seven. The letters run in order. If capital A is sixty-five, then B is sixty-six, and so on. To find a code, count on from the one you are given. Unicode is a much bigger character set. Its encodings use more bits for many characters, sixteen or more, so it can cover almost every language, plus symbols and emoji. Its first one hundred and twenty-eight codes match ASCII.

Here is a real question from Paper 1 in June 2022. Explain one reason to use Unicode instead of ASCII for languages other than English. It is worth two marks. The point: languages such as Arabic, Chinese or Urdu use many characters that are not English letters. ASCII has only seven bits, which is one hundred and twenty-eight codes. That is enough for English, but not for them. The reason: Unicode uses sixteen or more bits, which gives sixty-five thousand five hundred and thirty-six codes or more. So every character can have its own code. This explanation is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is saying standard ASCII uses eight bits, with two hundred and fifty-six characters. That is extended ASCII. Standard ASCII is seven bits. Another mistake is counting one too many. H is seven letters after A, not eight. Count the steps between, not the letters.

Small e is four steps after small a, so ninety-seven plus four is one hundred and one. One hundred and one is sixty-four plus thirty-two plus four plus one. The answer is one one zero zero one zero one.
