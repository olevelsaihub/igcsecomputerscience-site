Knowledge card

Protecting software by law: copyright, patents, trademarks and licences

Written

Copyright protects the code and is automatic, a patent protects an invention, a trademark a brand. A licence sets how software may be used. Buying software buys a licence, not ownership.

  • Intellectual property (IP) is a creation of the mind, such as software, music or an invention, that belongs to its creator.
  • Copyright protects the actual code and other written or creative work; it applies automatically as soon as the work is created, without having to apply.
  • A patent protects a new invention or technical process; it must be applied for and lasts for a fixed time.
  • A trademark protects a brand name or logo, such as a product's name.
  • A licence is a legal agreement that sets out how software may be used; buying software usually buys a licence to use it, not ownership of it.
  • Proprietary licences do not allow users to view, change or share the source code.
  • Free and open-source (FOSS) licences allow users to study, change and share the code.
  • Creative Commons licences let creators allow sharing and reuse on conditions they choose.
  • Software can be protected from illegal copying with licence keys and online registration or activation.

Worked explanation

  1. Paragraph 1: what intellectual property is and that Akiko's programs count as hers.
  2. Paragraph 2: copyright is automatic; mention patents and trademarks for inventions and names.
  3. Paragraph 3: licensing, including licence keys and online registration to stop illegal copies.
  4. Paragraph 4: choice of licence type (proprietary, open-source, Creative Commons).
  5. Conclusion: combining legal rights and licence controls.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Akiko's programs are her intellectual property: they are original creations that belong to her, so the law lets her control who copies or sells them.

Her code is protected by copyright automatically as soon as she writes it, so she does not need to apply. Anyone who copies or distributes her programs without permission is breaking the law, and she could take legal action. If her software includes a genuinely new technical process she could apply for a patent, and she could register a trademark so nobody else can sell products under her software's name.

Licensing controls how buyers may use the software. People who buy it only get a licence to use it, not ownership. She can sell each copy with a unique licence key, and require online registration or activation, so a copied version will not work on another computer.

She can choose the type of licence. A proprietary licence stops users changing or reusing her code. If she wants others to improve it, she could release it under an open-source licence, or use a Creative Commons licence that allows sharing on the conditions she sets.

Overall, copyright gives Akiko legal rights over her work, and licence keys and licence terms make those rights practical by preventing and discouraging illegal copying.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked. The answer covers both bullets (intellectual property and licensing), develops each point with how it protects Akiko's software, and ends with a conclusion, which fits the top level (5-6 marks).

Answer plan

  1. Note the two bullet points you must use: intellectual property and licensing; keep using Akiko's name.
  2. Intellectual property: define it, say copyright is automatic, explain what it lets her do (stop copying, take legal action); mention patent or trademark briefly.
  3. Licensing: buying software means buying a licence; describe a proprietary licence and what it forbids.
  4. Technical enforcement: licence keys, online registration or activation, supplying compiled code only; explain how each stops illegal copies.
  5. Balance: open-source or Creative Commons if she wants to share, with conditions she controls.
  6. Conclude: combine legal protection with the right licence and activation checks.
