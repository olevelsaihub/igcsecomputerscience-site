Knowledge card

Ethics of technology: privacy, inclusion and professionalism

Written
  • Inclusion means everyone being able to use technology; barriers include cost, no internet or devices where they live, lack of skills, disability, or simply not wanting to.
  • Some people avoid online services because they do not trust them, worry about hacking, or would rather talk to a real person.
  • Privacy is a person's right to control information about themselves.
  • Surveillance systems such as cameras with facial recognition collect personal data, so the people affected should give consent and know how it will be used.
  • Data protection laws require organisations to keep personal data secure, use it only for stated purposes and not keep it longer than needed.
  • Facial recognition can be biased, making more mistakes for some groups of people.
  • Computing professionals show professionalism by behaving legally and ethically, following a code of conduct, testing their code properly and keeping their skills up to date.
  • Professionals should respect users' privacy, avoid bias, and make software accessible to everyone.

Before you begin

  • What personal data is.
  • Basic idea of hacking and data security.
1

The method

  1. Decide which ethical area it is: inclusion, privacy or professionalism.

    Why: Each area has its own set of creditable points.

  2. Make each point specific to computing and to the scenario.

    Why: Generic points such as 'do their job' are not credited.

  3. For Give, write different reasons, not the same reason twice.

    Why: Each distinct reason earns one mark.

  4. For a privacy Discuss, cover consent, how data is stored and secured, who can see and use it, and possible bias, with a reason for each.

    Why: Levels marking rewards a range of developed, scenario-linked points.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Giving a generic job duty such as 'writing code' as professionalism.
  • ✗
    Giving two reasons that are the same idea, such as 'no money' and 'too expensive'.
  • ✗
    In a privacy Discuss, writing about cost or technical set-up instead of privacy.
  • ✗
    Making claims about the law with no link to the people being monitored.
  • ✗
    Listing words such as 'consent, security' with no development.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Plan four privacy ideas: consent, storing and securing the data, how the data could be used, and bias.
  2. For each idea, say what the issue is and why it matters for the employees.
  3. Weigh up: the facility has real security reasons, but these must be balanced against privacy.
  4. Finish with a conclusion.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The cameras would collect images of people's faces, which is personal data. Employees and visitors should be told about the system and asked for their consent, and the facility must have a clear legal reason for gathering this information.

The video and face data would need to be stored securely, for example encrypted and with access limited to a few named staff. If hackers stole it, people's identities and movements could be exposed, so the facility would be responsible for protecting it and for deleting it when it is no longer needed.

Monitoring every movement could be misused: managers might analyse how long staff take on breaks or who they talk to, which goes beyond keeping the site safe. A clear policy should say what the data may be used for and who is accountable if it is misused.

Facial recognition software can make more mistakes for some groups of people, so some employees might be wrongly flagged as being in restricted areas, which is unfair.

On the other hand, the facility may have valuable research to protect, and the system could stop unauthorised people entering restricted rooms. Overall, it should only be installed if consent, security, limited use and fairness are all properly handled.

How the marks are earned

Levels-marked. The answer covers several distinct privacy issues (consent, secure storage, misuse and accountability, bias), develops each with a reason linked to the facility, and gives a balanced conclusion, which fits the top level (5-6 marks).

June 2024 · Paper 1 · Q4(f)

Answer plan

  1. Note the focus: privacy issues in deciding whether to install cameras with facial recognition; mention Peter, Marie or the site.
  2. Point 1: faces are personal (biometric) data; data protection rules, telling people, consent.
  3. Point 2: proportionality; monitoring restricted areas vs tracking every movement; effect on trust.
  4. Point 3: how the data is used and who is accountable; risk of using it for other purposes; retention and deletion.
  5. Point 4: keeping the data secure from hackers; possible bias or misidentification.
  6. Show both sides: the security benefit the facility wants vs the privacy cost to staff.
  7. Conclude: install only if limited, transparent, secure and legal.
